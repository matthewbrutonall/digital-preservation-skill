# Providing Access to Preserved Digital Content

The central question: how do you make preserved digital content discoverable and usable without creating unnecessary legal, ethical, or technical risk?

The core message is simple: **access is part of preservation, not an optional extra**. If users cannot find, open, understand, or responsibly reuse content, preservation has not finished the job.

---

## Module 1: Starting with User Needs

Do not begin with a portal, a viewer, or a platform. Begin with users.

Questions to ask:
- Who are the users: staff, researchers, students, public users, depositors, auditors?
- What are they trying to do: read, watch, listen, verify, quote, download, reuse?
- What technical confidence do they have?
- Are they working remotely, on site, or both?
- Do they need item-level access, or will collection-level guidance be enough at first?

Ways to gather evidence:
- Survey current and potential users
- Review reference enquiries and help-desk requests
- Interview staff who support users now
- Run simple usability testing on guidance or discovery routes

Outputs from user-needs work:
- Priorities for what to make available first
- Decision on whether access copies are needed
- Level of descriptive metadata required
- Level of staff mediation required
- Accessibility requirements

Good practice means building the access offer your users can actually use, not the most technically impressive one.

---

## Module 2: Accessibility

Accessibility is not only about legal compliance. It is about whether people can use what you provide.

Practical points:
- Use accessible web pages, forms, and guidance
- Prefer common, well-supported access formats where possible
- Provide captions, transcripts, alt text, or descriptive guidance when appropriate
- Avoid workflows that depend on a mouse, a single browser, or specialist knowledge
- Write guidance in plain language

Accessibility also includes:
- Language barriers
- Low digital confidence
- Limited bandwidth or older hardware
- Reliance on assistive technologies

If the preservation master is hard to use, that is often a reason to create an access copy rather than forcing users to manage the original format.

---

## Module 3: Resource Discovery

Users cannot ask for content they do not know exists.

Minimum viable discovery:
- Collection-level description
- Clear title and scope statement
- Date range
- Basic information on formats or software dependencies
- Contact route for access requests

As capacity grows, add:
- Catalogue integration
- Finding aids
- Item-level metadata where justified
- Search or browse interfaces

Discovery metadata does not need to be perfect on day one. It does need to be good enough for users to understand what exists and how to request it.

---

## Module 4: User Guidance

Guidance reduces staff time and helps users succeed on the first try.

Good user guidance should explain:
- What the user is getting
- Whether it is a preservation master or an access copy
- What software is needed
- Any restrictions on copying, quotation, or reuse
- Whether support is available on site or remotely
- What to do if files do not open

This is the "informed download" model: users get files plus enough context to use them responsibly.

Good practice is to assume that many users will not recognise file extensions, codecs, or software dependencies without help.

---

## Module 5: Sensitive Data

Access decisions must consider privacy, confidentiality, safeguarding, and legal restriction.

Typical risks:
- Personal data
- Special category data
- Personnel or case files
- Commercially sensitive information
- Security-related information

Actions:
- Review the material before release
- Restrict, redact, or mediate access where necessary
- Record the decision and the reason
- Set review dates for closed or restricted files

Preservation and access are not the same decision. You may preserve material in full while only providing mediated or partial access.

## Module 6: IPR and Rights

You need a workable understanding of rights even when the legal position is not completely clear.

Questions to resolve:
- Who owns copyright or related rights?
- Do transfer or deposit terms allow preservation actions such as copying or migration?
- Do you have permission to provide access, and under what conditions?
- Are there licence terms for software, datasets, or born-digital artworks that affect access?

Good operational practice:
- Capture rights information during accession and transfer
- Use deposit agreements to cover preservation copying and format migration
- Record uncertainty rather than leaving rights fields blank
- Distinguish between preservation rights and public reuse rights

IPR uncertainty does not always stop preservation work, but it can change how access is provided.

**Jurisdiction note:** Copyright and data protection law varies by country. The guidance above is general; always apply the law of your jurisdiction. Key references by region:
- **UK**: Copyright Designs and Patents Act 1988; UK GDPR / DPA 2018; Freedom of Information Act 2000
- **Ireland**: Copyright and Related Rights Act 2000; GDPR; Freedom of Information Act 2014
- **USA**: Copyright Act §108 (library/archive exception); HIPAA for health data; state FOI laws; no single federal privacy law equivalent to GDPR
- **Australia**: Copyright Act 1968; Privacy Act 1988; various state FOI legislation
- **EU**: EU GDPR; national copyright implementations; Directive (EU) 2019/790 (Digital Single Market Directive), including out-of-commerce works provisions for cultural heritage institutions

When advising on rights for a specific jurisdiction, confirm which legal framework applies before drawing conclusions.

## Module 7: Access Copies

Access copies are derivatives created to improve usability and reduce risk to the preservation master.

Reasons to create them:
- The original format is obscure or hard to open
- The original file is too large or too fragile for routine use
- Accessibility is improved by conversion
- The original contains content that needs redaction or mediation

Examples:
- TIFF master -> JPEG or JPEG2000 access copy
- WAV master -> MP3 access copy
- High-resolution video master -> compressed streaming file
- Complex office document -> PDF or PDF/A access version when appropriate

Rules of thumb:
- Keep the preservation master unchanged
- Document what was created, when, and from what source
- Label access copies clearly
- Do not overwrite originals with converted versions

Access copies are for use. Preservation masters are for safekeeping.

---

## Module 8: Access Workstation

An access workstation is often the most realistic early access model for born-digital material.

It should:
- Use common, well-supported software
- Open the formats users are most likely to request
- Prevent alteration or deletion of preserved content
- Keep logs and clear operating procedures
- Allow staff assistance where needed

It is especially useful when:
- Rights or data protection concerns rule out remote delivery
- Files depend on local software
- Collections need supervision during use
- Access is currently occasional rather than high-volume

Start with a practical, supportable workstation before designing a complex portal.

---

## Module 9: Access Models and Service Design

Adrian Brown's five models are a useful way to scale service:
1. **Copy and forget** - provide the files with minimal support
2. **Informed download** - provide files plus technical guidance
3. **Viewer-friendly formats** - provide normalised or derivative access versions
4. **Provide viewer** - give users a player, workstation, or embedded environment
5. **Provide reuse tools** - enable richer interaction, extraction, or transformation

The DLF Levels of Born-Digital Access offer another staged approach across:
- Accessibility
- Description
- Research support
- Security
- Tools

Level 1 is a sensible baseline for many organisations: users can discover the material, request access, and use it with support.

---

## Practical Access Checklist

- Define who the users are
- Identify legal and ethical constraints
- Decide what level of discovery metadata is realistic
- Decide whether access copies are needed
- Prepare user guidance
- Check accessibility of the route you are offering
- Keep preservation masters separate from user-facing derivatives
- Record rights and restriction decisions

Good practice is to build a safe, understandable access service and improve it over time.
